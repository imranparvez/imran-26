Senior Product Designer - Design Manager · Atlassian forge

Unblocking apps for customers to move to cloud

Atlassian's customer were being pushed to cloud. Atlassian decided to end of life server in October 2020. On-premise customers, even if they wanted to move, were blocked. An atlassian customer is never truly just using an Atlassian product, they usually combine it with many marketplace apps. The marketplace apps they were using on their on premise installation was not yet available on the cloud. Marketplace partners, even if they wanted to build the apps to make a business on Cloud, were blocked too. With my core team I lead a 3 year strategy to unblock customers to move to cloud. We went from 9% to 83% customers unblocked. By the end of 2023 we had 189 out of 200 apps available.

About the product

Forge is Atlassian’s cloud app development platform, allowing developers to host apps on infrastructure that is provisioned, managed, monitored, and scaled automatically by Atlassian. Forge provides a complete toolkit for extending Atlassian apps.

The challenge in front of me

Atlassian on premise solution was fully extensible. Customers and Partners had full control of the source code, they could extend and build integrations however they like. However on cloud, we were just rolling out a new platfrom to build apps - Forge. There were 2 experimental forge modules when I was joining the team as the first designer on the team.

There were no guidelines of how to build forge modules.

Context

Jira is nothing without the apps

Enterprise on premise customers have very unique setups with several marketplace apps, integrations and privately built apps. Atlassian mandated the customers to move to cloud. Apps became the number 1 blocker to move to cloud because the apps that they were using on premise were not available for cloud.

Partners cannot build apps for Jira Cloud

Partners who built apps for Jira on premise had the freedom to build whatever they like. But on cloud they were blocked because the platform wasn’t ready.

Process

Phase 1: Mapping the problem through workshops

I designed and ran app analysis workshops where the team systematically examined the top marketplace apps that weren’t available on Cloud. For each app, the team did the following:

  1. Installed it on a local Jira Server instance
  2. Documented the major use cases it served
  3. Identified what specifically prevented the partner from rebuilding it on Cloud
  4. Created a blocker issue for each missing capability

This wasn’t a one-off research sprint. It was a sustained analysis of 400+ apps across multiple quarters, with a structured process that the whole team could follow. I wrote the analysis guide and workshop instructions so the process scaled beyond me.

Phase 2: Building the prioritization system

A recurring problem was visibility. Nobody not PMs, not engineering leads, not executives could answer basic questions: How many apps are blocked? What are they blocked on? Which blockers, if resolved, would unblock the most apps?

I pushed for creating a dedicated Jira project (the APP project) as a single source of truth. Each app got an issue. Each issue tracked its migration status through a workflow I designed:

StatusMeaning
TodoNo assessment yet
In progressAssessment underway
UnblockedNo known blockers for migration
BlockedActive blocker exists (linked to BLOCKER project)
AvailableCloud version exists, no migration path yet
MigratableCloud version exists with published migration path
Won’t migrateNative functionality, not suited for Cloud, or partner opted out

A separate BLOCKER project tracked each missing platform capability. Linking blockers to apps created an automatic prioritization signal - resolve the blockers linked to the most apps, and you unblock the most partners.

This system changed how teams across Atlassian coordinated on migration. Here’s some feedback regarding this from the team retrospectives:

“Building out APP Project brought so much visibility into the global state of app unblocking, has increased collaboration and touchpoints between cross-functional teams.”

“The specific app-by-app approach via APP project worked well for visibility and state of play.”

Phase 3: Designing the Forge extensibility

The analysis from workshops and the BLOCKER project produced a clear map of what Cloud was missing.

I designed the Forge modules that addressed these gaps. Over FY21–FY24, we went from 2 experimental modules to 30 Jira Forge modules, including the two most impactful ones unique to Forge: UI modifications and Forge Custom Fields.

Deep dive into top modules

UI modifications

UI Modifications (UIM) - a Forge module that allows third-party apps to change how Jira fields behave at runtime on the Global Issue Create view, Issue View, and Transition View.

One of the top apps that we were trying to unblock for Jira cloud was Scriptrunner behaviours. The app specialized in changing fields in run time, for example: when the field priority is set as high, make the due date field required.

What makes UIM special as a design case study is that it’s no-ui-design. I was designing a system where third-party code modifies native UI that Atlassian team didn’t build. It runs in an invisible iframe, with no direct visual output of its own. You’re designing an API that is the product - there are no pixels to push, only behaviors to define.

The module was designed as a platform capability, not a one-off solution for ScriptRunner. This meant any partner could build behaviours-style apps, which led to an entirely new app category on the Marketplace: field behaviour and issue template apps. Below lets take a look at some of the most important design decisions

Decision 1: “Do we inform the users for changes made by apps?”

Context: One of the behaviours we shipped was onChange. onChange allows partners to listen to changes of value of any field and react to it. For example: When user changes priority, run a pre-configured behavior. Lets assume the pre-configured behaviour makes the assignee to be set to team lead. Now we had to decide if we are going to notify the users when the assignee is set to team lead.

What was decided : When onChange resolves I decided: don’t show any notification just silently update the field.

Why this is interesting for platform design: This is a deliberate anti-pattern - most design guidelines say “always confirm state changes.” I argued that informing users would create more confusion than clarity, because users didn’t initiate the change knowingly. The app is doing what the admin configured. Drawing attention to it undermines the seamlessness.

Decision 2: “Should we allow users to interact with the fields while UIM is still running”

Context: When users want to create an issue, UIM can be configured to run some pre-configured behaviours on it. Lets assume that when issue type bug is selected and description field is filled with a template. There can be cases when the UIM configured there didn’t run instantly. Would we allow customers to edit the description or would we block the field. In the gif below you can see that when the user was typing out their description suddenly they lose everything and a template appears from UIM. This can happen because of the delay in UIM execution.

What was decided: I decided to disable those fields which UIM is trying to modify until the pre configured behaviour is run.

Why this matters: Designing onChange touched various platform questions:

  1. How does Jira know which fields will be affected before the app finishes running?
  2. Should fields remain interactive while an app is still processing?
  3. What does “touched” mean across different field types and editing modes (setVisible, setName, setDescription, setValue, setRequired)

This led to creating platform changes in which we request a list of fields that UIM is going to affect on the issue operations like create, view or edit. The decision was made to disable to the fields until they are ready to be interacted by the user. I saw that if users could continue editing fields while an app was still computing changes, the result was often not flexibility but ambiguity: values changed unexpectedly, admin rules partially applied, and the final state of the form was hard to reason about. In some cases, this produced exactly the kind of “wrong data and horrible UX” we were trying to avoid.

For me, this was an important design lesson. My original instinct was to allow users to interact with the fields while UIM is still running. I believed that when they are creating an issue we shouldn’t be hindering their progress. The better answer was “design the system so conflict is less likely and there’s no work lost.”

Decision 3: “Allow issue creation even if onChange fails”

Context: Lets take the same example as decision 1, When user changes priority, run a pre-configured behavior. Lets assume the pre-configured behaviour makes the assignee to be set to team lead. Now lets take the scenario that onChange script failed to run for whatever reason. We now needed to make a decision whether to inform the user or silently fail.

What was decided: no partial execution, no retry, let the user continue. The error is informational, not blocking.

Why this matters: This is where the tension between admin intent and user agency is sharpest. The admin wanted the onChange to enforce a rule. It failed and the system chose the user’s side - they shouldn’t be blocked from doing their work because a third-party script broke. We allow the user to continue creating the form and avoid interrupting them.

Impact:

  1. In 2024 - Forge hit a major milestone of 1000 apps built on Forge platform. In comparison it took 7+ years for Connect, the previous developer platform to reach that milestone.
  2. Built 30+ forge modules for partners to extend apps and unlock new business opportunities.
  3. We went from 9% customers unblocked to 83% customers unblocked by end of 2023. We had 189 out of top 200 apps available.
  4. At the end of 2024 there were 9000+ installs of apps that use the module UI modifications, making it the most popular business category on marketplace.

Reflections

Team growth

I joined as the first and only designer Jira extensibility team. Over the course of the project, I went from Senior Product Designer to Design Manager.

I hired and built a team of 3 product designers and 2 content designers. This was a very gradual increase over 3 years. I built up business case for each and every role and provided evidence for their need to be able to reach our goals. One of the designers in this team also grew from Mid-level to Senior.

What didn’t work: extensibility guidelines

We attempted to create guidelines for how extensibility should work across Forge - a principled framework partners and internal teams could follow. It didn’t land. This really had to do with the goal of unblocking apps. We were not trying to build a forge extensibility model, we were trying to unblock already existing extremely popular apps. These apps were complex and rarely had something common.
