Design Manager · Atlassian Marketplace

Building a delivery system for a team in transition

In H1 FY25 I took on the Marketplace design team during an Atlassian-wide org change. Two designers and our manager left in the same quarter. Three new designers joined. Our scope expanded from one product to the entire Marketplace org. In the six months that followed, the team of five shipped Aether, App Editions, Multi-instance Licensing, 150k Support, and Marketplace Progressive Handover — and stayed one sprint ahead of engineering on every workstream.

This is about the process I built to make that possible, what failed in the first attempt, and what I'd take with me to the next team.

About the product

Atlassian Marketplace is the third largest revenue-generating business at Atlassian. When I joined, the org was navigating a period of declining monthly recurring revenue. My team focused on initiatives with direct, near-term impact on MRR.

Walking into the whirlwind

When the playbook doesn’t fit, build a new one.

I moved to a Atlassian Marketplace in August 2024 to lead one of the pillars in it. Within few weeks Atlassian India org change was announced. This led to two of my team mates getting impacted. The following week the Head of Design for Marketplace also moved on from Atlassian. This left me as the interim Head of Design for Marketplace. My scope expanded from one pillar in an org to entire org that is 3rd most revenue generating for Atlassian org. All this in a matter of 3 weeks.

When I met my cross-functional partners, they were vocal about design velocity. Since I just joined the team, I had no visibility into who was working on what and barely understood the space too. Even with that some of the things were quite clear: the team had no shared backlog, no shared definition of done and no shared cadence. Engineering was running on sprints. Product was running on quarters. Design was running on best effort.

1. Centralising the work

Three things had to change.

My first attempt was a copy of the engineering ritual stack: sprint kickoff, mid-sprint check-in, async planning, a Confluence page as the source of truth.

It collapsed within three cycles. Two reasons:

Kickoffs were theatre. Engineering teams kick off because they’re working on the same feature. My designers were spread across five initiatives with almost no overlap. The kickoff turned into five monologues, each one irrelevant to four people in the room.

Confluence was the wrong shape. A page with a list of tasks doesn’t tell you what’s blocked, what’s at risk, or what’s next. The backlog grew faster than anyone could prune it. Designers stopped updating it. Stakeholders couldn’t read it.

I scrapped both and rebuilt around two principles: rituals should produce a decision, and the system of record should make priorities visible to people outside the team.

A Jira structure modelled on how design moves

We modelled the work in three layers:

  • Epics — initiatives owned by a designer
  • Tasks — work an individual could complete alone
  • Sync tasks — work requiring at least one other person

Co-creating estimation with the seniors

Two senior designers led the estimation framework. We landed on story points sized by amount, complexity, and risk, with worked examples for product and content design tasks.

The framework itself wasn’t the point. Co-creating it was. The team owned the calibration, so the team trusted the numbers. It also gave the two seniors a piece of work more durable than any one project — a team-wide standard with their fingerprints on it.

Collapsing three rituals into one

The mid-sprint check-in survived. Everything else got cut. The check-in had one job: surface blockers and align on approach.

To stop it from devolving into a series of 1:1s with the manager, I changed the rules:

  1. Senior team members were expected to ask questions and offer help on every update — not just their own. This wasn’t optional. The check-in became the place where the team practised being a team.
  2. Every blocker became a teaching moment for the whole group. When one designer was holding designs back from engineering until they were “polished,” I used the check-in to reframe progressive handover as a team norm, not a critique of one person.

Retro feedback from the team:

“Sprint planning — we delivered a lot of work, with such a small team.” (2 votes)

“Visibility of my tasks. What I need to do next and where should I add updates.”

2. Synchronising with the org

Process matters. Visibility makes it stick.

A working sprint inside the team is worth nothing if cross-functional partners can’t see it. Three practices closed that gap.

Sub-org leadership syncs

I set up a recurring sync with leadership in each sub-org (customer-facing and partner-facing). The agenda was always the same: what should design focus on in n+2? These conversations let us reprioritise before a sprint started, not during it.

When a PM asked us to take on a new initiative mid-sprint, I’d open the Jira board, show what was already committed, and walk them through the trade-offs. The PM would then negotiate with the owner of the work being displaced.

The leadership move was small but it mattered: the prioritisation conversation moved off my plate and onto theirs. Trade-offs are owned by the people who own the priorities, not by design.

Broadcasting the n+1 plan

At the end of every sprint, I posted to our cross-functional Slack channels:

  1. What we shipped this sprint
  2. What we’re picking up next sprint
  3. “Reach out now if you need design support in n+1.”

Three sprints in, the message stopped being news and started being expected. Partners began planning around it.

Broadcast until people listen

At Atlassian scale, one announcement is never enough. I learned to repeat the message until it stopped needing repeating. When a commit ticket landed mid-sprint and a partner expected designs by Friday, I’d reply with the same n+1 framing — same words, same link — until the pattern was internalised.

Peer feedback

“We needed better coordination between design, product, and engineering teams as previously the work was being done in parallel, causing inefficiency. Imran streamlined the working of the design team and started working in sprints. He made sure design is at least one sprint ahead of engineering, so engineering is not blocked by design. He also brought transparency to design bandwidth so dev sprints can be planned accordingly. The result: better sprint planning where product, design, and engineering teams coordinate better.”

“Great way to organise the priorities. This seems more proactive and aligned to engineering priorities.”

“Improves visibility across the team and cross-functional peers. Cross-functional team appreciates the visibility of the work we’re doing.”

3. Moving craft upstream

The best time to influence quality is before the work starts.

The unexpected payoff of running a real backlog: it became the most effective tool I had for raising design quality.

Once each initiative had a backlog of tasks, I could read the backlog and ask one question — will these tasks add up to a great outcome? If a critical research step was missing, I added it. If a task was over-scoped, I split it. If something wasn’t worth doing, I cut it.

This shifted my role as a manager. Instead of catching quality issues at design review (too late, sunk cost), I was shaping the path to the solution while the path was still being drawn. The same backlog that gave stakeholders visibility gave me leverage on craft.

It’s the move I’d port to any team I led next. Most managers spend their craft energy at review. The leverage is upstream.

Reflections

The job is to own everything that touches the team, even the parts you didn’t break.

The version of this process that worked wasn’t the version I started with. The first attempt borrowed too much from engineering and too little from how design actually moves. The second worked because the team co-built it — the estimation framework, the ritual cadence, the definition of an epic — so they had skin in the calibration.

If I were starting again tomorrow, I’d skip the Confluence step and go straight to a Jira board co-designed with the team in week one. I’d run a single ritual until I had evidence I needed a second one. And I’d treat the backlog as a design quality tool from day one, not as an after-effect.

The bigger lesson is one I didn’t have words for at the time. The job is to own everything that touches the team, even the parts you didn’t break.

Each of the problems I built a system for had an upstream cause I could have pointed at. Progressive handover was a behaviour I could have left with one designer. The velocity perception was partly an org problem the whole India org was absorbing. The prioritisation conversations weren’t really mine to have. I built systems around all three anyway — and the team stabilised because of it. The year-without-attrition number was an ownership outcome.

That’s the move I’d take to the next team. Treat any problem in the team’s path as design’s problem to solve, regardless of who introduced it.

I entered Marketplace during a turbulent quarter. I left the team running on rails. Thank you to my team for trusting me with the task of leading the team during a time of transition.
